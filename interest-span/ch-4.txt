Here's something particularly interesting.

Not everyone who withdraws from a relationship does so because they want to hurt the other person.

Some people simply struggle with confrontation.

Imagine realizing that you're no longer interested in someone.

You know that an honest conversation is necessary.

But you also know that the conversation might involve uncomfortable questions, disappointment or emotional reactions.

Instead of addressing the situation directly, you start creating distance.

You reply less frequently.

You stop initiating conversations.

Eventually, you disappear.

This behaviour is commonly associated with avoidance, although it would be inaccurate to diagnose someone's attachment style from a few unanswered messages.

Research published in 2024 by psychologists YeJin Park and Nadav Klein examined the motivations behind ghosting.

Across several experiments, they found that people who ghosted sometimes believed they were avoiding hurting the other person's feelings.

Interestingly, the people being ghosted often underestimated these other-oriented motives.

But here's the problem.

Even when someone disappears with the intention of avoiding discomfort, the other person is still left without an explanation.

An action intended to avoid hurting someone can end up creating a different kind of hurt.

And good intentions don't automatically make poor communication harmless.
